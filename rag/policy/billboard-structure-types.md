# Billboard structure types

Every billboard on BillboardBD has one structure type, chosen when the owner lists it:
unipole, multipole, wall, gantry, rooftop, freestanding, static, backlit, frontlit, led or
neon. This describes what each one is and how the closely-related pairs differ.

## Unipole

A single-pole structure: one central pole holding up one billboard panel above it. This is
the classic tall roadside billboard shape. Because the whole panel's weight and wind load
rests on one pole, unipoles are usually a moderate size rather than very large.

## Multipole

The same idea as a unipole, but the panel is held up by two or more poles instead of one.
Multipole structures are used when a panel is larger or heavier than a single pole can
safely support, or where local wind load needs extra support. The panel itself is not
fundamentally different from a unipole's — the difference is entirely in how many poles
carry it.

**Unipole vs multipole, in short:** the number of supporting poles — one vs several. A
multipole board is generally chosen for bigger panels; a unipole for smaller, simpler ones.

## Wall

An advertisement mounted or painted directly onto the side of an existing building wall,
rather than on its own free-standing structure. No pole or frame of its own — it borrows
the building it's on.

## Gantry

A structure that spans across a road, mounted on an overhead frame (like a bridge) so the
advertisement faces traffic passing underneath it. Used on highways and major roads where
a roadside panel would be less visible than one directly overhead.

## Rooftop

A billboard mounted on top of a building rather than at street level. Common in dense areas
where there is no room for a ground structure but a building roof gives height and
visibility.

## Freestanding

A standalone ground structure that is not a simple single pole (unipole) or multi-pole
frame — its own independent base and support, not attached to any building. Used as a
catch-all for structures that do not fit the other named shapes.

## Static

A traditional printed billboard: one fixed image or design, printed on vinyl or a similar
material and mounted on the panel. It does not change or animate — "static" refers to the
content being fixed, not to the supporting structure (a static board can be a unipole,
multipole, wall, or any other structure type).

## LED

A digital display billboard, built from LED screens instead of a printed panel. Because
it's a screen, an LED board can show changing or animated content rather than a single
fixed image — this is the main thing that sets it apart from a static board.

## Neon

A sign that uses neon-style glowing tube lighting to form its design, most often used for
smaller shop-front or storefront signage rather than large roadside panels.

## Backlit vs frontlit — how a panel is lit at night

These describe how a static (printed) panel is illuminated, not the structure holding it
up.

**Backlit** — the panel's printing material is translucent, and the light source sits
*behind* the panel, shining through it. This gives an even, glowing look across the whole
panel and reads clearly at night, but it needs special see-through print material and a
light fixture built into the structure behind the panel.

**Frontlit** — ordinary (non-translucent) print material, lit by external lights — usually
spotlights — mounted in front of the panel and aimed at its face. This is simpler and
cheaper to build than backlit, but the lighting is less even across the panel and the light
fixtures themselves are visible in front of the sign.

**In short:** backlit shines light *through* the panel from behind it; frontlit shines
light *onto* the panel from in front of it.
